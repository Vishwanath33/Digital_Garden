A blog is a stack. The newest thing is on top, everything else sinks, and a
post from two years ago is understood to be a historical artifact — you do
not edit it, you write a new post saying you changed your mind, and the two
sit in the archive contradicting each other with no indication of which won.

This is a strange way to organise thinking. Almost nothing I care about is
best understood in the order I happened to think of it.

A garden inverts the defaults:

- **Topic over chronology.** Notes are found by subject and by what links to
  them, not by when they were posted. The [index](/notes/) is sorted by date
  only because it has to be sorted by something.
- **Revision over accretion.** When I learn something, I change the note. The
  note is the current state of my thinking; the git history is the record of
  how it got there.
- **Visible immaturity.** A blog post implies finishedness. A note marked
  *seedling* does not, so it can be published at a stage where a post could
  not be. See [[epistemic-status|epistemic status]].
- **Density of links.** The value is in the connections. Notes here link to
  each other constantly, and every note lists what links back to it, so the
  structure is legible from anywhere in it.

## The obvious objection

If everything is revised in place, the reader cannot tell what changed, and a
garden becomes a way to quietly be right in retrospect.

This is a real problem and the answer is mechanical rather than moral: the
whole site is a git repository, every note has a *History & source* link at
the bottom, and every edit is a public commit. I cannot silently revise. I can
only revise.

{% aside "Cost" %}
The honest downside: gardens are much worse than blogs at telling you
*what is new*. A regular reader of a blog knows where to look. A regular
reader of a garden has to be told. The feed here publishes on tending, not
just on planting, which helps a little and is not a full answer.
{% endaside %}

## Why it survives

Mostly because the alternative failed. I kept a private notes system for
years and it accumulated about four thousand fragments that no one, including
me, ever read again. Writing for an audience — even a hypothetical one —
forces a fragment to become a sentence, and a sentence to have a subject.
The public part is not vanity. It is the constraint that makes the note
worth keeping.

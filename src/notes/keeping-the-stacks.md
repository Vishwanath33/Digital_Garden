Most writing about personal knowledge bases is about why to have one. Very
little is about the upkeep, which is the part that decides whether one
survives its first season. These are working rules, held loosely.

## Revise on contact, not on schedule

The moment to correct an entry is when it comes up in something else: a
book, an argument, another entry. That is when the new information actually
exists and the friction of editing is lowest. Revision done on a schedule
tends to be cosmetic.

## Split when a section starts arguing

An entry should make one claim. When a section inside it grows its own
thesis, its own objections and its own sources, it has become an entry and
should be shelved separately and cited. The tell is usually a sentence that
begins "but of course".

## Withdraw visibly

Some entries turn out to be wrong, or to be about nothing. Librarians call
removing a book *deaccessioning*, and the good ones keep a record of it. Do
the same: demote the stage, replace the body with a short account of what
was wrong and what superseded it, and keep the address alive so incoming
citations still land. A withdrawn entry that explains its own withdrawal is
more useful than a 404.[^linkrot]

[^linkrot]: This also means never reusing an entry's address for a
different subject, which is the one form of link rot entirely within the
library's control.

## Cataloguing is the work

When adding an entry, the useful question is not "is this good" but "where
does it go, and what does it cite". An entry with no backlinks after a month
usually belonged inside an existing entry as a paragraph. The graph makes
this visible: an isolated node is a question worth asking.

## Open questions

Genuinely unresolved, which is why this entry is still a manuscript:

- Should the feed announce every revision, or only substantial ones? Every
  revision is honest and noisy.
- Is there a size at which cross-referencing makes a library less navigable
  rather than more? [[a-library-not-a-blog|A library, not a blog]] assumes
  linking scales. That is an assumption, not a finding.
- How much should an entry assume the reader has read the entries it cites?
  Writing each one standalone causes enormous repetition.
